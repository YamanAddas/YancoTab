2 · The Psychology of Action

When we encounter an object, we don't read instructions — we use the cues the object itself gives us. A door with a flat plate affords pushing; a door with a vertical handle affords pulling.

Yet all around us we find doors that contradict their own affordances — push plates next to "PULL" signs, handles on doors that swing outward.

"When a simple object needs an instruction manual, the design has failed."

The mismatch between perceived and intended action is what Norman calls the Gulf of Execution.

Across this gulf the designer must build a bridge — through visible signifiers, mappings that match user expectations, and feedback that confirms each action…

The Seven Stages

Action proceeds through seven approximate stages. They aren't always sequential, and a single act may compress them all into a heartbeat — but they explain why some interfaces feel obvious and others feel like fighting the machine.

  1. Forming the goal
  2. Forming the intention
  3. Specifying the action
  4. Executing the action
  5. Perceiving the world
  6. Interpreting the perception
  7. Evaluating the outcome

The final stage — evaluation — closes the loop. Without it the user cannot know whether the act succeeded; the system feels broken even when it isn't.
